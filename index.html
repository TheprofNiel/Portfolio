My Story

The Engineer Who Learned to Watch the Gaps

I didn't start out wanting to manage projects. I started in Computer Engineering, learning how systems are built, piece by piece, line by line. But the real lesson wasn't in the code. It was in watching what happens when nobody owns the space between the parts. A backend team assumes the frontend team knows something. A deadline moves and three people find out three different ways. Small cracks, left alone, become the reason a whole project falls apart.

That's what pulled me toward project management. Not the title, the pattern.

The moment I knew this was the work I wanted to do was watching a project drift, slowly, quietly, because no one was tracking the small things. Nobody was lying or slacking off. There just wasn't a system catching the details before they became problems. I stepped in, built that system, and watched the chaos turn into something people could actually rely on. That feeling, structure replacing panic, is still the part of this job I chase every time.

"Delivering the software" stopped being the finish line. It was never really the point.

Somewhere in those early projects, my definition of success changed. The point was always whether the business problem got solved, whether the thing we built actually moved the needle for the people who needed it to.

That shift is the lens I bring into every engagement now. I track timelines before they slip, not after. I flag blockers the moment I see them, not when they've already cost someone a week. I document as I go, so nothing lives only in my head. And I measure success the way the business feels it, not just what's checked off a list.
